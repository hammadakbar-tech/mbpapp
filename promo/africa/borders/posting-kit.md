- **Chapter II:**
  - Berlin 1884: "AFRICANS INVITED: 0".
  - The Scramble colours the map by colonial power until only Ethiopia and Liberia are free.
  - Lord Salisbury's 1890 quote.
  - Split peoples: the Maasai and the Somali.
- **Chapter III, the twist:** a 2024 study found most borders followed older frontiers, rivers and lakes; the straight lines are mostly in deserts. This gives the video a "more accurate than other videos" angle.

  - Countries switch to their own colours year by year (1960 = 17).
  - 1964: keep the borders.
  - Eritrea 1993, South Sudan 2011.
- **Ending:**
  - Today: 54 countries; Nigeria has 500+ languages.
  - "Should Africa's borders be redrawn? YES / NO".

## Reply ideas
- **"The Berlin Conference divided Africa!"** → *It set the rules (e.g. "effective occupation"). Most actual lines were drawn in treaties between the powers over the next 30 years.*
- **"Why not just redraw them?"** → *In 1964 African states agreed to keep them, because redrawing could trigger endless border wars. Most leaders still back that today.*
- **"What about Western Sahara?"** → *Its status is disputed, which is why it's hatched on the map. It isn't counted among the 54 UN member states.*
- **"Ethiopia was colonised by Italy!"** → *Italy occupied it from 1936 to 1941, but Ethiopia was never a colony during the Scramble and won at Adwa in 1896.*

## Fact check
- **54 countries:** all are UN members. Western Sahara (disputed) and Somaliland (self-declared, not recognised) are not counted.
- **1870, ~10%:** the standard textbook figure is about 10% of Africa under European control in 1870, mostly coastal. That rose to about 90% by 1914.
- **2,000+ languages:** Ethnologue lists over 2,000 languages for Africa. Nigeria has about 520.
- **Berlin Conference (Nov 1884 – Feb 1885):** 14 states attended (13 European countries and the USA, with the Ottoman Empire among them). No African representatives were present. It set rules for claims; it did not draw most borders itself.
- **1914:** Ethiopia and Liberia were the only fully independent states.
- **Salisbury:** in a speech in 1890, Lord Salisbury said "We have been engaged in drawing lines upon maps where no white man's foot ever trod…". He was Prime Minister at the time.
- **Straight lines:** Barbour (1961) estimated that 44% of African borders are astronomical (latitude/longitude) lines. The glowing lines in the video are long straight border sections detected automatically from Natural Earth data.
- **Split peoples:** the Maasai live on both sides of the Kenya–Tanzania border. Somali people lived across five colonial-era territories (British and Italian Somaliland, French Somaliland/Djibouti, Ethiopia's Ogaden, Kenya's Northern Frontier District), which is the five-pointed star on Somalia's flag.
- **2024 study:** Paine, Qiu & Ricart-Huguet, "Endogenous Colonial Borders" (APSR, 2024) found that pre-colonial frontiers and natural features shaped most borders, and that straight lines were common mainly in sparsely populated areas such as deserts.
- **1960:** 17 African countries gained independence (the "Year of Africa").
- **1964:** the OAU's Cairo resolution committed states to respect the borders that existed at independence.
- **New countries:** Eritrea became independent in 1993 and South Sudan in 2011; South Sudan is the world's newest UN member state.
- **Map:** colonial colours show the main power in 1914 for each modern country, simplified (e.g. Zanzibar and Spanish Morocco are not shown separately). Borders are approximate.
